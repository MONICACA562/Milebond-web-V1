Who Owns the Mold in China? A $30,000 Case Study

The case in 90 seconds. The client is an Amazon FBA seller. His product is a rechargeable Electric Fly Swatter Racket. He met Supplier A at the Canton Fair, placed orders directly with them, and paid $30,000 in tooling fees to open his private molds. MileBond was not involved in any of that — no sourcing, no order placement, no tooling payment. He ran everything on his own until the relationship collapsed. Only after the price hikes, the white-label leak, and the profit crash did he come to us and ask us to fix it. This is what happened, and the rules we now give every seller so it never happens to them.

Who owns the mold if I pay the tooling fee in China?

Short answer: in Chinese manufacturing industry practice, the buyer who paid the tooling fee owns the mold — "who pays, who owns." Treat that as a widely accepted business custom, not an absolute legal guarantee you can enforce with one phone call.

In a mold ownership China dispute, what actually decides the outcome is your evidence. Four things form the proof threshold:

  1. A written mold ownership agreement — separate from the purchase order, bilingual, stamped with the factory's company chop.
  2. Payment records annotated as tooling — a wire transfer memo that says "tooling fee for [product name]" is worth far more than a generic "payment."
  3. Invoices and receipts that itemize the mold separately from unit production.
  4. Physical evidence — dated photos of the molds, serial numbers, engraved ownership tags, and the mold drawings.

Our client had exactly one of the four: a wire record showing $30,000. He had met Supplier A at the Canton Fair, shaken hands, and wired the tooling fee with no separate agreement on mold ownership. That's not a hopeless position — but it's a weak one, and the factory knew it.

Can a Chinese factory legally keep my molds or use them for other clients?

Two different questions, two different answers.

Keeping your molds: Without a contract, some factories argue the tooling fee only covered "mold development and first production use," not ownership. Whether that argument holds up legally is almost beside the point — while lawyers debate it, the factory physically holds the steel, and possession is leverage. "Factory kept my mold" stories almost always start with a missing agreement.

Using your molds for other clients: This is a clear breach of industry norms and of basic private label mold protection principles — full stop. Injection mold ownership means nothing if the factory runs your cavities overnight for a competitor. But enforcement through Chinese courts is slow and expensive, which is exactly why it keeps happening.

In this case, near-identical fly swatter rackets started appearing under other brands, retailing $2 below his own listing. Same housing texture. Same handle seam. When your design shows up on a listing you don't control, that's not a coincidence — that's your mold, running for someone else.

Why did the factory raise prices — and why did the seller feel trapped?

The mechanism is depressingly predictable. When his orders were small, Supplier A was friendly and flexible. When his volume surged, the factory realized he was dependent — and the excuses started rotating: resin costs went up, labor costs went up, the exchange rate moved, capacity was tight.

His unit price climbed from $3.80 to $4.25. His margin compressed from 38% to 17%. On a product selling thousands of units a month, that's the difference between a growing brand and a slow bleed.

Why didn't he just leave? Because his $30,000 molds were sitting in Supplier A's workshop. Re-cutting the molds elsewhere meant another $30,000, another 45–60 days of tooling lead time, and the risk of going out of stock mid-transition and tanking his Amazon ranking. The molds were, functionally, hostages.

One thing to be precise about: through all of this, the seller was operating alone. MileBond had no role in his sourcing, his Canton Fair deal, or his tooling payment — we didn't even know him yet. He came to us after the margin collapse, when he needed someone on the ground in China to get him out. Our job started at the problem, not at the beginning.

How do you actually recover molds from a factory?

Recovering molds from a factory is logistics plus evidence, not drama. Here's what we did:

  1. Built the evidence pack first. Wire records showing the $30,000 tooling payment, his invoices, product photos, and his mold drawings. You never walk in asking "can we discuss who owns the mold" — you walk in to collect property you can document.
  2. Showed up in person. We're based in Yiwu, so we drove to the factory ourselves. A mold dispute with a Chinese factory reads very differently on email than it does across a table, in Chinese, with paperwork in hand.
  3. Ran a documented handover. Every mold set was inventoried on-site: serial numbers photographed, condition recorded, count confirmed against his drawings.
  4. Arranged the physical move. Truck, loading, and transport out the same visit — leaving "to come back later" is how molds get "lost."

Total time on-site: 6 hours. Firm, procedural, no shouting. Factories rarely fight a prepared, documented retrieval — they fight vague requests from people 8,000 miles away.

What happens when molds have been worn by unauthorized use?

When we inspected the recovered molds, the story of the white-label runs was written into the steel: visible cavity wear, flash forming along the parting line, worn ejector pins, and maintenance that clearly hadn't followed any schedule. Unauthorized production runs don't come with authorized maintenance.

We sent the molds to an independent third-party tooling shop for assessment and repair. Here's what they found and fixed:

What are the 5 mold management rules every Amazon seller needs?

Everything in this case was preventable. These are the five rules we now walk every client through:

  1. Sign a separate mold ownership agreement before you pay the tooling fee. Not a clause buried in a purchase order — a standalone bilingual agreement, chopped by the factory, covering ownership, exclusive use, and retrieval rights.
  2. Pay tooling with a traceable paper trail. Annotate the wire transfer as a tooling fee for the specific product. Demand an itemized invoice. Keep the mold drawings. If you ever have to prove ownership, you'll do it with documents, not memories.
  3. Register your molds like assets. Serial numbers, engraved ownership tags, dated photos of every mold set, and a simple asset register you control. Factories treat tagged, photographed property differently.
  4. Audit usage — don't just trust reports. Ask for production logs. Do the capacity math: cavities × cycle time × shifts should roughly equal your output. If a factory runs your volume in half the calculated time, your molds may be running someone else's orders the rest of the time. Unannounced visits keep everyone honest.
  5. Pre-plan your exit. Your agreement should guarantee mold retrieval within a set notice period. And have someone local who can physically show up — a mold management sourcing agent on the ground turns a months-long email fight into a same-day truck.

A solid mold ownership agreement should contain at minimum:

Exclusive useNo third-party production without written consentStops white-labeling
RetrievalBuyer may collect molds within X days of noticePreserves your exit leverage

FAQ

Who owns the mold after paying the tooling fee in China?

Industry practice is "who pays, who owns" — but that's a business custom, not an automatic legal guarantee. Enforceability depends on your paper trail: a signed mold ownership agreement, tooling-annotated payment records, and itemized invoices. Without them, the factory's physical possession becomes the deciding factor.

Can I get my molds back if the factory refuses to return them?

Usually yes, but expect negotiation, not a quick email reply. Arrive with evidence — payment records, drawings, photos — be firm and procedural, and arrange the transport logistics on the spot. A local representative who can show up in person dramatically improves the outcome.

How much does mold repair cost compared to building a new mold?

Typically a small fraction. In this case, repairing the wear from unauthorized use cost $1,800 versus $30,000 to rebuild — roughly 6%. Always inspect before deciding: worn cavities and ejector systems are usually repairable.

What should a mold ownership agreement include?

Five clauses at minimum: ownership, exclusive use, retrieval rights with a notice period, maintenance obligations, and liquidated damages for breach. It should be bilingual, stamped with the factory's company chop, and reviewed by a China-qualified lawyer.

How do I stop a factory from running my molds for other brands?

Contract first — an exclusive-use clause — then verify continuously: production logs, capacity math, surprise visits, and serial-tagged molds. If a leak happens, document it and move production rather than chasing damages through slow legal channels.

Is paying by wire transfer enough proof that I own the mold?

It helps, but it isn't decisive on its own. Annotate the transfer as a tooling fee, keep the invoice and mold drawings, and get a standalone ownership agreement signed. Wire records plus an agreement is a strong position; wire records alone is a weak one.
