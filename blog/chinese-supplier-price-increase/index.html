Your Chinese Supplier Raised the Price 15% — Don't Switch Until You Read This

A price increase isn't just about the unit price. Here's how to calculate the total inventory cost and decide whether switching actually saves you money.

You've been ordering the same product from the same supplier for eight months. The price has been $4.50 per unit. Then one Monday morning you get an email:

"Due to rising raw material costs, effective next order the price will be $5.18 per unit."

That's a 15% increase. On 2,000 units a month, that's $1,360 extra per month. $16,320 per year.

Your first instinct: find a new supplier. You open Alibaba, send out 20 RFQs, get 8 replies. Three quote $4.20 — cheaper than your original price. You're ready to switch.

Don't. Not yet.

Because the unit price is only one line item in the true cost of working with a supplier. Switch to that $4.20 supplier and you might discover their MOQ is 2,000 instead of 500, they require 50% deposit instead of 30%, their lead time is 60 days instead of 25, and their defect rate is 8% instead of 2%.

Six months later, you're paying more total cost than if you'd just accepted the 15% increase — and you've lost eight months of relationship with a supplier who actually delivered.

This is the mistake almost every small buyer makes when a supplier raises prices: they compare unit price instead of total inventory cost.

The Total Inventory Cost Framework

When you're evaluating whether to accept a price increase or switch suppliers, you need to look at five factors, not one:

FactorWhat It Costs You
Unit priceDirect product cost per unit
MOQHow much cash you must tie up per order; how much dead inventory risk you carry
Payment termsHow long your cash is locked up before you can sell the product
Lead timeHow much safety stock you must carry; how often you stock out
QualityDefect rate, return rate, Amazon account health risk

A supplier who is $0.30 cheaper per unit but requires 4x the MOQ, 20% more deposit, 2x the lead time, and 3x the defect rate is not cheaper. They're more expensive — you just can't see it in the unit price.

Let me show you how this works with real numbers.

Real Case: A 15% Price Increase That Was Actually a Good Deal

The client: an FBA seller doing home goods, 2,000 units/month of a best-selling SKU.

The original supplier (Supplier A):

  • Unit price: $4.50
  • MOQ: 500 units
  • Payment: 30% deposit, 70% before shipment (after 6 orders, moved to Net 15)
  • Lead time: 25 days
  • Defect rate: ~2% (verified by third-party inspection, consistently passing AQL 2.5)

The price increase: Supplier A raised the price to $5.18 (+15%), citing raw material cost increases.

The alternative (Supplier B): found on Alibaba, quoted $4.20 per unit (-7% vs original price). But:

  • MOQ: 2,000 units (4x higher)
  • Payment: 50% deposit, 50% before shipment (new supplier, no relationship)
  • Lead time: 50 days (2x longer)
  • Defect rate: unknown (no track record; sample looked fine but samples always look fine)

The client's instinct: switch to Supplier B and save $0.30/unit.

What we calculated:

Cash tied up per order:

  • Supplier A (at new price): 500 units × $5.18 = $2,590, paid Net 15 (cash tied up ~15 days)
  • Supplier B: 2,000 units × $4.20 = $8,400, 50% deposit = $4,200 due at order, $4,200 due before shipment (cash tied up ~50+ days)
  • Supplier B ties up $5,810 more cash per order

Safety stock required:

  • Supplier A (25-day lead time, reliable): ~30 days of safety stock = 2,000 units
  • Supplier B (50-day lead time, unproven): ~75 days of safety stock = 5,000 units
  • Supplier B requires 3,000 more units in safety stock = $12,600 more inventory

Defect risk (estimated):

  • Supplier A: 2% defect rate = 40 units/month = $184/month in losses (at $5.18)
  • Supplier B (industry average for unproven supplier): 5-8% defect rate = 100-160 units/month = $420-$672/month in losses
  • Supplier B likely costs $236-$488 more per month in defects

The verdict: Supplier B's $0.98/unit "savings" ($5.18 - $4.20) is completely consumed by higher cash tying, more safety stock, and higher defect risk. On a total inventory cost basis, Supplier A at the increased price was actually cheaper than Supplier B at the lower price.

The client stayed with Supplier A. We used the price increase as an opportunity to renegotiate other terms: got MOQ reduced from 500 to 300, and got Net 15 extended to Net 30. The net effect: unit price up 15%, but cash flow improved because of lower MOQ and longer payment terms.

This is the counterintuitive truth about supplier price increases: sometimes accepting the increase and using it as leverage to improve other terms is better than switching to a "cheaper" supplier who costs you more in every other dimension.

The 4-Step Response to a Price Increase

Step 1: Demand a Written Justification and Verify It

When a supplier raises prices, your first move is not to negotiate. It's to ask:

"Please provide a written breakdown of the cost increase. Which raw materials increased, by what percentage, and what is the date of the increase? Please attach the supplier's invoice from their raw material vendor."

This does three things:

  1. It separates real increases from fake ones. If the supplier can't produce a vendor invoice with a company stamp, the "raw material increase" is probably a margin grab. We've seen suppliers cite "plastic price increases" when the actual plastic resin price had been flat for six months.
  2. It gives you data to negotiate. If the raw material is 40% of the product cost and it went up 10%, the justified price increase is 4% — not 15%. You can push back on the gap.
  3. It creates a paper trail. If this goes to a dispute later, you have written evidence of what the supplier claimed.

How to verify: check the raw material price on a commodity index. Plastic resin, steel, aluminum, cotton — all have public price indices. If the supplier says "steel went up 20%" and the index says steel went up 5%, you know you're being played.

The last three rows are where the real comparison happens. Most buyers stop at the first row (unit price) and make a bad decision.

Cash tied up per order = (MOQ × unit price) × (payment term factor). For 30/70 payment, the factor is ~1.0. For Net 30, the factor is ~0.5.

Safety stock required = daily sales × (lead time + safety buffer). For a reliable 25-day supplier, buffer = 5-7 days. For an unproven 50-day supplier, buffer = 20-25 days.

Monthly defect cost = monthly units × defect rate × unit price. For a known supplier, use your actual inspection data. For a new supplier, assume 5-8% until they prove otherwise.

Step 3: Negotiate — But Not Just on Price

If the total inventory cost table shows your current supplier is still competitive (even after the increase), don't just accept the increase silently. Use it as leverage:

"I understand raw material costs have increased, and I'm willing to accept a price adjustment. But in exchange, I'd like to discuss: (1) reducing MOQ from 500 to 300, (2) extending payment terms from Net 15 to Net 30, (3) locking this price for 6 months."

Suppliers are much more willing to improve non-price terms when they're getting a price increase. They've already won the price battle; giving you a lower MOQ or longer payment terms costs them relatively little and keeps you as a customer.

What to ask for (in order of impact):

  1. Longer payment terms (Net 15 → Net 30) — biggest cash flow impact
  2. Lower MOQ (500 → 300) — reduces cash tying and dead inventory risk
  3. Price lock period (6 months at the new price) — protects you from another increase
  4. Free upgrades (better packaging, free inspection, faster lead time) — lower cost elsewhere

If the supplier refuses all of these and just demands the price increase with no concessions, that's information too — it tells you they don't value your business, and switching may be the right call.

Step 4: If You Switch, Do It Slowly — With a Test Order

If the total inventory cost table shows an alternative supplier is genuinely cheaper (not just on unit price but on total cost), don't switch 100% immediately. Do this:

  1. Place a small test order (200-300 units) with the new supplier. Pay for a third-party pre-shipment inspection. Verify quality, packaging, lead time, and communication.
  2. If the test order passes, place a medium order (500-1,000 units) while still ordering from the original supplier. Run both in parallel for 2-3 months.
  3. Compare actual performance (not quoted performance) — real lead time, real defect rate, real communication responsiveness.
  4. If the new supplier outperforms on total cost, gradually shift more order volume to them. Keep the original supplier as a backup at 20-30% of volume — never go 100% with a new supplier in the first year.

This slow transition costs you a little bit in the short term. But it protects you from the #1 risk of switching suppliers: discovering after 3 months that the "cheaper" supplier has 10% defects and 60-day lead times, and you've already burned the bridge with your original supplier.

The Hidden Cost of Switching That Nobody Mentions

There's one more cost to switching that doesn't show up in any spreadsheet: relationship capital.

You've spent 8 months with Supplier A. They know your product specs, your packaging requirements, your quality standards, your shipping preferences. They know that if you say "ship by Friday" you mean Friday, not "sometime next week." They know your inspector's name and they don't try to cut corners because they know you check.

A new supplier has none of this. The first 3-6 months with a new supplier are a learning curve for both sides. Miscommunications happen. Specs get misinterpreted. Quality slips because they don't yet know your standards.

We estimate that the first 6 months with a new supplier typically costs an additional 5-10% in operational friction — rush orders, rework, miscommunications, emergency air freight. That's on top of the defect risk and lead time uncertainty.

Factor this into your total inventory cost calculation before you switch.

The Bottom Line

A supplier price increase is stressful, but it's also an opportunity — to audit your true cost structure, to benchmark against the market, and to renegotiate terms you wouldn't otherwise have leverage for.

The worst response is a panic switch to the cheapest Alibaba quote you can find. The best response is: verify the increase, calculate total inventory cost across all five dimensions, negotiate for non-price concessions, and if you do switch, do it slowly with a test order.
