How to Pay 1688 Without a Chinese Bank Account: 4 Methods Compared for 2026

You found the perfect supplier on 1688. Then you hit the payment wall. Here are the 4 actual ways to pay from overseas — with real fees, real limits, and the scams we fell for so you don't have to.

Get Payment Help
11 min read · Updated Sep 2026

1688 is Alibaba's wholesale platform — prices are 20–40% lower than Alibaba.com for the same goods. But there's one problem: most suppliers only accept RMB via Alipay, WeChat Pay, or Chinese bank transfer.

If you're reading this, you already know the pain. You've tried Alibaba Trade Assurance (they want USD but prices jump). You've asked the supplier — "Alipay only." You've researched PayPal (most 1688 suppliers don't have a business account).

We've tested every viable workaround. Here are the 4 methods that actually work in 2026, compared head-to-head.

The 1688 Payment Wall, Explained

1688 runs on Alipay's domestic payment rails — designed for Chinese buyers only. Cross-border payments aren't blocked by law; they're just not enabled by default. Most suppliers haven't turned on international payment settings because:

  • Compliance paperwork. To accept foreign payments, suppliers must register with the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE) — most small factories skip this.
  • Currency conversion risk. RMB pegging to a basket means daily fluctuation; many prefer to avoid FX volatility.
  • 1688's fee structure. Suppliers pay 2–5% extra on cross-border orders, so they push domestic-only payment.
Important. The payment wall is not unfixable. About 15–20% of active 1688 suppliers now have cross-border pay enabled — you just need to know how to find them, or work around the ones that don't.

Method 1: 1688 Cross-border Pay (KJB) — Direct, Low-Cost, But Not Universal

1688's official cross-border payment system is called KJB (跨境宝). It's built into the platform — you pay in your local currency (USD, EUR, AUD, GBP, etc.) and Alipay exchanges it to RMB for the supplier. This is the cleanest method when it works.

Step-by-step for KJB Cross-border Pay:

  1. Create a standard 1688 account (use your foreign phone number — 1688 accepts international numbers now).
  2. Find a supplier with the "跨境宝" badge on their storefront.
  3. Add items to cart → proceed to checkout.
  4. Select "Cross-border Pay" (跨境支付) as the payment method.
  5. Pay via Visa, Mastercard, or international Alipay. Currency conversion happens automatically.
  6. After payment, the order is protected by 1688's buyer protection — same as domestic orders.

KJB Pros & Cons:

  • Pros: Integrated into 1688; buyer protection built in; transparent 1–2% fee; no Chinese bank needed.
  • Cons: Only ~15–20% of suppliers have it enabled; some categories (custom orders, machinery) rarely offer KJB; daily limits vary by supplier (often ¥50,000–200,000 RMB).

Method 2: WorldFirst World Account — RMB Accounts for Foreign Businesses

WorldFirst (now part of Ant Group, the same company that owns Alipay) lets foreign businesses open a World Account — a multi-currency account with local receiving details in 10+ currencies, including RMB. You fund the account in USD/EUR, exchange at interbank rates, and pay suppliers via domestic Alipay transfer from WorldFirst.

Step-by-step for WorldFirst:

  1. Apply online at worldfirst.com — takes 1–3 business days for business accounts.
  2. Receive your account with RMB receiving details.
  3. Fund via wire transfer from your bank or card.
  4. Exchange USD/EUR to RMB in the dashboard (spot rate + 0.3–0.7% spread).
  5. Use the RMB balance to pay suppliers via Alipay — enter their Alipay ID or phone number.

WorldFirst Pros & Cons:

  • Pros: Works with ALL 1688 suppliers (even those without KJB); excellent FX rates; legitimate regulated institution; supports bulk payments.
  • Cons: Business account verification required (personal accounts limited); ~0.3–0.7% FX spread + ¥15–25 per transfer; supplier must accept Alipay domestic transfer.

Method 3: Specialized Cross-border Payment Services (Zyla, Topbuyer)

Several third-party services have built direct integration with 1688's APIs. Zyla is the most established — you connect your 1688 account, add items to cart inside Zyla's dashboard, and they handle the RMB payment to the supplier on your behalf.

Zyla and Similar Services:

  • How it works: You give Zyla a one-time read-only access token to your 1688 account. They "walk through" the checkout on your behalf and pay the supplier using their network of Chinese payment rails.
  • Fees: Typically 3–5% of order value, sometimes with a minimum.
  • Limits: Varies by service; Zyla supports up to ¥500,000 RMB per order.

Caution with These Services:

Red flag. Never provide your 1688 password directly — legitimate services only ask for a temporary access token. Also verify they don't add hidden FX spreads on top of the stated percentage fee.

Method 4: Fee-Only Buying Service — Simplest, But Pricier

A fee-only buying service (like MileBond) does the whole purchase for you. You send a product list; they source from 1688, negotiate, place orders using their domestic Alipay account, consolidate shipments, and forward internationally. You pay a transparent fee (usually 5%) on top of the actual invoice cost.

Why This Works for Many Buyers:

  • No need to open foreign accounts or apply for anything.
  • Eliminates the supplier communication barrier (most 1688 suppliers speak minimal English).
  • Consolidation = lower per-unit shipping.
  • Quality control can be added at the factory or warehouse.

Common Scams to Avoid

The payment method isn't the only risk. Watch for these:

  • Supplier asks for personal Alipay transfer outside 1688. "Let's skip the platform and you save 2%." No buyer protection = no recourse if they don't ship.
  • "Discount" for Western Union. Same problem — untraceable, irreversible. Any legitimate supplier accepts Alipay through 1688.
  • Fake "WorldFirst agents" on WeChat. They'll offer to "set up your account" for a fee. WorldFirst never charges setup. Always apply directly at their official site.
  • Third-party services holding your funds. Some smaller payment services require a deposit; if they're unregulated, your money can disappear overnight.
